# Paper Summary 04

**Lambert, S. I., Madi, M., Sopka, S., Lenes, A., Stange, H., Buszello, C.-P., & Stephan, A. (2023). An integrative review on the acceptance of artificial intelligence among healthcare professionals in hospitals. *npj Digital Medicine*, 6, 111.**

## Summary

An integrative review synthesising studies on what drives healthcare professionals' acceptance of AI in hospital settings. The authors organise influencing factors into levels: individual (AI knowledge and literacy, prior technology experience, perceived usefulness, fear of replacement or deskilling, perceived threat to professional autonomy), technological (reliability, transparency, workflow integration, usability), and organisational/environmental (training provision, leadership support, involvement of end users in implementation, ethical and legal clarity). Recurrent findings: insufficient AI knowledge breeds distrust; involvement of clinicians in design and rollout consistently improves acceptance; concerns about accountability and liability suppress it. The review concludes that acceptance is not a fixed attitude but is shaped by organisational strategy, and recommends structured education and participatory implementation as the most actionable levers.

## Key factors identified

- **Individual:** AI literacy, perceived usefulness, autonomy threat, role-replacement fear
- **Technological:** reliability, transparency, interoperability/workflow fit
- **Organisational:** training, leadership endorsement, user involvement, ethical/legal clarity

## Relevance to this research

The strongest existing bridge between individual attitudes and the organisational level, which makes it the empirical anchor for RQ3. Its multi-level structure is adopted in the conceptual framework (`frameworks/`). It also justifies surveying managers and digital leads alongside clinicians, not clinicians only. However, nearly all primary studies in the review concern predictive or task-specific AI; whether the same factor structure holds for generative tools (which professionals often adopt bottom-up, informally, and without organisational sanction) is precisely the open question. Informal "shadow adoption" of consumer LLMs by clinical staff is a phenomenon the review's hospital-deployment framing does not capture, and the interview guide probes it directly (Q4).

## Critique / gaps

- Integrative reviews inherit heterogeneity; constructs like "acceptance" and "trust" are measured inconsistently across primary studies.
- Hospital-centric; primary care, community, and private-sector healthcare organisations are underrepresented.
- Pre-generative evidence base.
