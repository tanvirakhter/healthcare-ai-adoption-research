# Research Questions v0.1

Topic: Trust, Explainability, and Adoption of Generative AI Systems in Healthcare Organisations

Design logic: RQ1 establishes the trust mechanism, RQ2 tests the most-claimed lever (explainability), RQ3 moves from individual to organisational level. Together they cover formation, intervention, and institutionalisation.

---

## RQ1 - Trust formation and calibration

**How do healthcare professionals form, calibrate, and lose trust in generative AI systems, and how does this process differ from trust formation in predictive AI?**

Sub-questions:
- RQ1a. Which system properties (fluency, accuracy, consistency, source grounding, uncertainty signalling) act as trust cues for generative AI?
- RQ1b. How do professionals respond to first-hand experience of hallucination: graded recalibration or categorical rejection?
- RQ1c. Does linguistic fluency inflate trust beyond what system reliability warrants (over-trust via competence mimicry)?

Working hypotheses:
- H1a. Perceived reliability and information credibility are the strongest antecedents of trust (per TrAAIT), but their effect is moderated by AI literacy.
- H1b. A single salient hallucination experience reduces trust disproportionately to its statistical frequency (negativity asymmetry).

Method mapping: survey Q5-Q11 (quant), interview Q5-Q7 (qual).

---

## RQ2 - Explainability

**What forms of explainability influence trust and intention to use generative AI in clinical and administrative workflows, and under what conditions does explainability fail to help or actively mislead?**

Sub-questions:
- RQ2a. Do verifiable explainability mechanisms (source citation, retrieval grounding, confidence/uncertainty flags) affect trust differently from self-generated reasoning narratives?
- RQ2b. Does explainability adequacy predict intention directly, or only via trust?
- RQ2c. Are there user segments (by role, AI literacy, seniority) for whom explanations increase miscalibrated trust?

Working hypotheses:
- H2a. Verifiable mechanisms increase calibrated trust; effect size larger for clinical than administrative tasks.
- H2b. Explainability's effect on intention is fully mediated by trust.
- H2c. Self-generated reasoning traces increase trust without improving trust calibration (the Ghassemi critique, tested in the generative context).

Method mapping: survey Q12-Q13, interview Q8.

---

## RQ3 - Organisational adoption

**Which organisational factors mediate the relationship between individual trust and organisational adoption of generative AI in healthcare settings?**

Sub-questions:
- RQ3a. How do governance posture (permit/restrict/ignore), training provision, and leadership stance affect whether individual willingness becomes sanctioned use?
- RQ3b. How prevalent is informal, unsanctioned use of consumer generative AI tools ("shadow adoption"), and what does it indicate about the gap between individual and organisational trust?
- RQ3c. How do regulatory and liability interpretations (clinical safety standards, data protection, professional accountability) shape organisational decisions?

Working hypotheses:
- H3a. In organisations with restrictive policies and no sanctioned alternative, individual trust predicts shadow use rather than non-use.
- H3b. User involvement in implementation and structured training (per Lambert et al.) are the strongest organisational predictors of sanctioned adoption.

Method mapping: survey Q14-Q15 + demographics, interview Q3-Q4 and Q9.

---

## Scope decisions (to confirm with supervisor)

1. **Population:** healthcare professionals and managers in UK healthcare organisations (NHS and private). Rationale: single regulatory environment; access feasibility. Risk: limits generalisability claims to UK.
2. **System scope:** text-generating systems used in clinical and clinical-adjacent work (documentation, communication, information retrieval). Excludes image generation and drug discovery.
3. **Unit of analysis:** individual professionals (RQ1, RQ2) and organisations as embedded cases (RQ3).
